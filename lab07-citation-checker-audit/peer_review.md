# Technical peer review of the draft manuscript — Prompt 16 — 2026-11-17

*Reviewer stance: critical, written against the first compiled draft (`paper/main.pdf`, 9 pages) before any revision. The second half of this file is the revision log showing what changed in response.*

## Summary of the submission

The paper compares a DOI-only citation checker with two field-matching checkers on citations built from Crossref records. It reports that field matching catches every constructed error, that it falsely flags 54% of legitimate variants, that one author-rule change cuts this to 19.5%, and that the error definition alone moves precision by 40 points. The writing is clear and unusually honest about its limits. My concerns are mostly about whether the strongest-sounding results are as informative as they look.

## Major concerns

**M1. The B3 result is partly built in (Sections 3.2–3.3, 5.3).** The same person designed the "et al." stress variant and the containment rule that fixes it, both in amendment A1. The paper says the rule was written down before any held-out run, and the timestamps support that. But writing a test and its fix together guarantees the fix works on that test. That "B3 removes 30/30 et-al flags" is therefore not evidence that containment is a good rule. It shows the rule does what it was designed for. The real evidence is that B3 lost *no* recall elsewhere, and even that is weak, because no error category tests partial author errors.
*Why it matters:* readers will take "cut false flags from 54% to 19.5%" as a general improvement.
*Remedy:* say this explicitly in Section 5.3 and in the limitations, and describe the result as a demonstration of mechanism, not as validation of B3.

**M2. "Legitimate variants" are derived from registry fields, not observed citations (Section 3.2, Table 1).** The abbreviated venues are Crossref's own `short-container-title`, and the print-year variant is Crossref's own print date. Real reference lists abbreviate in other ways (ISO4, house styles, typos). The paper treats these 87 cases as "a way real reference lists differ", which overstates their realism.
*Remedy:* state that the variants are registry-derived approximations of real conventions, and that their prevalence (30 et-al, 11 venue, …) reflects which records allowed each variant, not how often real citations look like this.

**M3. The 54% headline rate depends on the mix of variant types.** The overall false-flag rate is a weighted mix of five quite different mechanisms. The weights come from applicability (39 hyphen breaks, which never fire, against 1 print-year case). A different mix would give a different headline number. That's acceptable if it's said, but the abstract and conclusion quote 54% and "more than half" without that caveat.
*Remedy:* add one sentence where the 54% first appears in the results, and lead with the per-mechanism numbers (Table 3), which don't depend on the mix.

**M4. RQ3 numbers are not in any table (Section 5.4).** The identity-only rescoring, one of the four claimed contributions, is reported only in prose.
*Remedy:* add identity-only rows to Table 2.

## Minor concerns

- **m1.** In Fig. 1, the dashed lookup arrow runs through the text of the first box.
- **m2.** References print the arXiv identifier twice ("arXiv preprint arXiv:… arXiv:…").
- **m3.** Section 4 says "about 100 requests". The cache and logs make an exact count possible: 16 baseline lookups, plus 90 in this phase (two `sample` calls because the first attempt crashed, 40 lookups, 40 synthetic-DOI checks and 8 test synthetic-DOI checks), so 106 in total.
- **m4.** Related work says Walters and Wilder examined "ChatGPT-generated literature reviews". The verified notes in `gap_matrix.md` only support "citations generated by ChatGPT" and the fabricated-versus-erroneous split. Don't describe their method beyond what was checked.
- **m5.** The bootstrap section doesn't say that stress cases per source vary (0–4; one work allowed none), so sources with more applicable variants weigh more in the stress-rate intervals.
- **m6.** Fig. 3 places "without title" between two points. The label is readable but could be clearer. *Not changed:* the caption plus the CSV make it unambiguous, and moving it caused new collisions.

## Things the paper does well (and should keep)

The paper separates preregistered results from the amendment, avoids treating zero-width intervals as precision, refuses to call the venue check useless when the design gave it nothing to find, and keeps prevalence claims out entirely. The reproducibility statement matches the repository.
