\begin{abstract}
People now use large language models (LLMs), and the ``deep research'' tools built on them, to find and cite scholarly and professional literature. These systems often cite sources that do not exist, credit real papers to the wrong authors, or link to URLs that lead nowhere. I call this citation fabrication. This review compares benchmarking studies from 2023 to 2026 in biomedicine, law, computer science, and general search. It covers four kinds of system: base LLMs such as GPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Bard/Gemini; citation-focused benchmarks such as ALCE and FActScore; retrieval-augmented professional tools, mainly commercial legal research assistants; and autonomous agents such as OpenAI, Gemini, and Perplexity Deep Research. The reported numbers are hard to line up. They run from about 3\% of URLs to 91\% of references, and much of that spread comes from differences in domain, prompt, and verification method. Plain chat models usually fabricate the most. Retrieval helps but does not solve the problem: commercial legal tools still gave hallucinated or incomplete citations 17--33\% of the time. Deep research agents have their own profile. They cite much more per query, and in the largest URL audit so far, 3--13\% of URLs from commercial models and agents were hallucinated while 5--18\% did not resolve. I also review detection and mitigation methods, along with the evidence that fabricated citations have reached peer-reviewed papers. The main weaknesses of this literature are inconsistent definitions of ``hallucination,'' the lack of shared cross-system benchmarks, and closed commercial pipelines. I end with directions that could make future results comparable.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}\label{sec:introduction}
Ask a chatbot for sources and it may hand back references that look perfect and are partly or wholly false. This matters now that people use large language models (LLMs) as research assistants. Citations are also a good place to study ``hallucination'' in general \citep{agrawal2023,ji2023}, because each one can be checked: the work either exists with the stated authors, venue, and year, or it does not.

The problem is already large. One audit covered 111 million references in 2.5 million papers on arXiv, bioRxiv, SSRN, and PubMed Central. It found a sharp rise in non-existent citations that tracks the spread of LLMs, and it conservatively estimated 146,932 hallucinated citations in the 2025 literature alone \citep{zhao2026}. Fabricated references have turned up in NeurIPS and USENIX Security proceedings, and even in some award-winning papers \citep{russinovich2026b}. They have also appeared in accepted NeurIPS 2025 papers \citep{ansari2026} and in hundreds of papers at ACL, NAACL, and EMNLP \citep{sakai2026}. Courts have seen the same thing. Case citations invented by ChatGPT led to sanctions in Mata v. Avianca, similar filings followed, and researchers began to study legal hallucination directly \citep{dahl2024,magesh2025}.

People also find citations in more ways than they did a few years ago. Retrieval-augmented generation (RAG) pipelines, ``AI research assistants'' inside legal and medical databases, and ``deep research'' products from OpenAI, Google, Perplexity, and others now run their own searches, read several web pages, and write long reports with inline citations \citep{du2025,rao2026}. Some vendors have sold retrieval as a way to make citations ``hallucination-free'' \citep{magesh2025}. That claim can be tested, so it should be.

This paper asks three questions. How common is citation fabrication across bare LLMs, citation-focused benchmarks, retrieval-augmented professional tools, and deep research agents? What methods can detect or reduce it, and how well do they work? What gaps stop the field from producing benchmarks that can be compared and trusted? Section~\ref{sec:methodology} explains how I selected and organized the evidence. Section~\ref{sec:background} covers related work. Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} compares fabrication rates by system class. Section~\ref{sec:mitigation} looks at detection and mitigation, and Section~\ref{sec:challenges} at the limits of the existing studies. Section~\ref{sec:gaps} sets out research gaps, Section~\ref{sec:synthesis} pulls the evidence together, and Section~\ref{sec:conclusion} concludes.

\section{Review Methodology}\label{sec:methodology}
I organized this review as an evidence synthesis, so that studies are compared with one another and not simply summarized in turn. It covers empirical studies and benchmarks from 2023 through September 2026 that measure whether citations exist, whether their bibliographic details are right, whether they support the claim attached to them, or whether their URLs work. The studies fall into four settings: general chat models, citation-grounded generation, domain-specific retrieval tools, and autonomous deep research agents.

A study was included if (i) it evaluated a named model, system, benchmark, or verification pipeline; (ii) it measured citation or source reliability directly or through a reproducible proxy; (iii) it gave enough methodological detail to interpret the metric; and (iv) it could be traced to a scholarly venue, publisher, or persistent preprint record. I excluded work that discussed hallucination only in concept, gave no citation-related evidence, or could not be located independently. These filters matter because the reported rates shift with the prompt, the domain, the sample size, and the definition of failure.

The analysis has three layers. \emph{Existence} asks whether the cited work or URL is real. \emph{Attribute accuracy} asks whether the authors, title, venue, year, and identifiers are correct. \emph{Support accuracy} asks whether the source actually backs the claim it is attached to. Keeping these apart stops a real but irrelevant citation from being counted the same way as an invented one. Figure~\ref{fig:review-workflow} shows the process.

\section{Background and Related Work}\label{sec:background}
\subsection{Hallucination as a general phenomenon}
In natural language generation, ``hallucination'' means output that sounds plausible but is unsupported by, or at odds with, the source material or known facts. \citet{ji2023} separate intrinsic hallucination, which contradicts a given source, from extrinsic hallucination, which the source can neither confirm nor deny. \citet{huang2023} later extended this work across dozens of detection and mitigation techniques. Citation fabrication is usually extrinsic. An LLM asked for supporting literature often has no source document at all, so it produces a plausible-looking entry from patterns in its training data \citep{agrawal2023}.

\subsection{Early documentation of citation fabrication in LLMs}
The first systematic evidence came from biomedicine. Researchers had ChatGPT draft short papers or literature summaries, then checked each reference by hand against Medline, Google Scholar, and PubMed. \citet{bhattacharyya2023} examined 115 references from 30 short medical papers written with ChatGPT-3.5: 47\% were fabricated, 46\% were real but had inaccurate details, and just 7\% were real and accurate. \citet{athaluri2023} saw a similar pattern in another sample. \citet{wagner2024} put 88 radiology questions from eight subspecialties to ChatGPT-3.5, and 64\% of the 343 resulting citations could not be found in PubMed or on the open web. \citet{mcgowan2023} asked ChatGPT and Bard for psychiatry references. Accuracy fell as low as 6\% for ChatGPT and 0\% for Bard's plain-text citations, although Bard's PubMed hyperlinks reached real articles 66\% of the time when queried repeatedly. The output format, in other words, can change the result. \citet{goddard2023} and \citet{emsley2023} questioned the word ``hallucination'' itself, since a fabricated citation is a statistical artifact and not a perceptual error. \citet{emsley2023} preferred the terms fabrication and falsification.

\citet{walters2023} ran the most thorough of the early studies. They used GPT-3.5 and GPT-4 to write 84 short literature reviews on 42 topics, giving 636 citations. GPT-3.5 fabricated 55\% of its citations and GPT-4 fabricated 18\%. Even among the real ones, 43\% of GPT-3.5's and 24\% of GPT-4's had substantive errors in authors, dates, volume or issue numbers, or publisher details. Pooling six comparable studies, the same authors reported a 51\% fabrication rate across 732 citations, with domain rates from 47\% to 69\%, and with geography worse than medicine. \citet{chelli2024} then tried systematic-review replication. ChatGPT-3.5, GPT-4, and Bard hallucinated between 28\% and 91\% of references depending on model and topic, and precision, meaning the share of references that were both real and relevant, never passed 13.4\%.

\subsection{Generative search engines}
A second line of work looked at generative search engines, which are built to answer with inline web citations. \citet{liu2023} had people evaluate Bing Chat, NeevaAI, Perplexity.ai, and YouChat. Only 51.5\% of generated sentences were fully supported by their citations, and only 74.5\% of citations supported the statement they were attached to. These two measures, citation recall and citation precision, capture different parts of what the authors call ``verifiability,'' and a system needs both. \citet{gao2023} turned citation-grounded generation into a benchmark task called ALCE. On the open-ended ELI5 dataset, even the best systems of the time lacked complete citation support for about half of their content.

\subsection{Domain-specific and agentic benchmarking}
Law has produced some of the strongest work, because case citations are unambiguous and well indexed. \citet{dahl2024} gave general-purpose LLMs, including GPT-4, structured legal queries and found hallucination rates of at least 58\%. The models were poor at predicting their own errors and often accepted users' false legal premises. Legal-technology vendors then promoted retrieval-augmented products as the answer, so \citet{magesh2025} ran the first independent, systematic audit of three of them: Lexis+ AI, Westlaw's AI-Assisted Research and Ask Practical Law AI, and Casetext CoCounsel. Each still produced hallucinated or incomplete citations between 17\% and 33\% of the time. That is a large improvement on bare LLMs, but it is a long way from ``hallucination-free.''

The newest studies look at agents that plan multi-step searches and write long reports. \citet{du2025} introduced DeepResearch Bench and scored systems including OpenAI, Gemini, and Perplexity Deep Research on effective citation count and citation accuracy. Perplexity had the highest accuracy at 90.24\%, while Gemini's agent produced the most effective citations per report. \citet{rao2026} offer the most direct comparison so far. They audited 53,090 citation URLs from 10 models and agents on DRBench, plus 168,021 URLs from 3 models across 32 academic fields on ExpertQA, and used the Internet Archive's Wayback Machine as an independent existence check. Between 3\% and 13\% of URLs were hallucinated, with no record of ever existing, and 5--18\% did not resolve once link rot was counted. Deep research agents gave substantially more citations per query than search-augmented LLMs and also hallucinated URLs at higher rates. Non-resolving rates varied by field, from 5.4\% in Business to 11.4\% in Theology.

\subsection{Contamination of the scholarly record}
A further strand of work shows fabricated citations entering the permanent literature, peer review included. \citet{zhao2026} audited 111 million references across four major repositories. Non-existent citations rose sharply after LLMs were adopted, mostly in fields with fast AI uptake and among smaller, early-career author teams. The hallucinated references also tended to credit scholars who were already prominent and predominantly male. \citet{russinovich2026b} built a verification pipeline called RefChecker (not the tool of the same name from \citet{hu2024}). They found that about one in twenty 2025 NeurIPS and USENIX Security papers had at least two likely hallucinated references that survived peer review, at an audit cost of about four cents per paper in one venue-scale scan. \citet{sakai2026} found nearly 300 papers with at least one ``HalluCitation'' across ACL, NAACL, and EMNLP in 2024--2025, concentrated in the most recent conference they sampled. \citet{ansari2026} manually analyzed 100 fabricated citations in 53 accepted NeurIPS 2025 papers, about 1\% of the total, even though three to five experts reviewed each paper. ``Total Fabrication'' made up 66\% of the cases in the resulting five-category taxonomy. A Nature news feature on this work concluded that hallucinated citations are now a measurable and growing contaminant of the scientific record \citep{naddaf2026}.

\section{Main Technical Discussion: A Comparative Taxonomy of Fabrication Across System Classes}\label{sec:taxonomy}
\subsection{Defining the failure mode}
We first need to be clear about what is being measured, because inconsistent definitions explain much of the variance in reported numbers (see Section~\ref{sec:challenges}). The literature points to at least four kinds of citation failure:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Total fabrication.} The cited work does not exist under any title, author combination, or identifier \citep{bhattacharyya2023,ansari2026}.
\item \textbf{Author-identity or attribute corruption.} A real work exists, but the authors, year, venue, volume, or page numbers are wrong enough that the citation no longer points to it \citep{walters2023,russinovich2026b}.
\item \textbf{Misattributed support (citation-support failure).} The source is real and correctly identified, but it does not support the attached claim. Verifiability metrics such as citation precision and recall target this failure \citep{liu2023,gao2023}.
\item \textbf{URL/link hallucination versus link rot.} A cited URL may never have existed, or it may have existed and later gone dead. Both look the same at audit time, so \citet{rao2026} separate them with an independent historical record, the Wayback Machine.
\end{enumerate}

\citet{russinovich2026b} also argue that ``ordinary bibliographic drift,'' such as venue or year differences between versions of a paper and minor name variants, should not count as hallucination. Counting it inflates the rates and hides the more serious identity-level failures.

The ``volume effect'' can be stated simply by separating the error probability per citation from the expected errors per report. If a system produces $N$ citations in a report, and the estimated probability that any one of them is fabricated is $p$, then the expected number of fabricated citations is
\begin{equation}
E[F] = Np,
\label{eq:volume-effect}
\end{equation}
assuming, as a first approximation, a common $p$ across citations. Equation~\eqref{eq:volume-effect} does not claim that citation errors are independent. Its point is that a percentage alone says little when agentic systems differ so much in how many citations they produce.

\subsection{What the pattern shows}\label{sec:pattern}
Table~\ref{tab:rates} shows three patterns. First, newer models fabricate less, but they still fabricate. In \citet{walters2023}, GPT-4's rate was about a third of GPT-3.5's on comparable tasks. Second, retrieval lowers fabrication without ending it. Commercial legal RAG tools cut hallucination by half or more compared with bare LLMs on similar tasks \citep{magesh2025,dahl2024}, and the best deep research agent in \citet{du2025} passed 90\% citation accuracy on curated tasks. Yet an independent check against the Wayback Machine still found 3--13\% hallucinated URLs across commercial systems \citep{rao2026}. Third, and most important for this paper, agents bring a different kind of risk. A deep research agent runs many searches and cites far more than a single chat turn, and \citet{rao2026} found that agents also hallucinate URLs at higher rates than search-augmented LLMs. In terms of Equation~\eqref{eq:volume-effect}, both $N$ and $p$ go up, so the expected number of fabricated citations per report grows on both counts. The same arithmetic warns against easy comfort: if a future agent did lower $p$, a large enough $N$ could still leave readers with as many bad citations as before. I think this ``volume effect'' is the most underexplored variable in agent benchmarks. It is also one more reason to doubt ``hallucination-free'' marketing, a claim that the audit by \citet{magesh2025} did not bear out.

\subsection{Differences between domains}\label{sec:domain}
Rates also change with the domain. \citet{walters2023} found geography worse than medicine, \citet{rao2026} found non-resolving rates from 5.4\% in Business to 11.4\% in Theology, and \citet{chelli2024} found wide variation between review topics within one medical subspecialty. My reading is that coverage in a system's training and retrieval corpus matters at least as much as any one architectural feature. If that is right, a legal-only or medical-only audit tells us little about a system's citation reliability in general.

\section{Current Approaches and Developments in Detection and Mitigation}\label{sec:mitigation}
\subsection{Self-verification}
\citet{agrawal2023} treat hallucinated book and article references as a ``model organism'' for studying hallucination. They show that a model can often expose its own invented references, with no external resource, when it is asked a structured series of direct and indirect questions about a reference's content and authors. The authors report high identification accuracy. Some fabricated references, it seems, come with internally inconsistent ``knowledge,'' so self-consistency can work as a cheap first filter, though only a partial one.

\subsection{Sampling and claim checking}
\citet{manakul2023} introduced SelfCheckGPT, a zero-resource, black-box method. It samples several generations for the same prompt and measures how well they agree, on the premise that invented details vary more between samples than grounded ones. \citet{min2023} introduced FActScore, which splits long generations into atomic claims and checks each against a trusted knowledge source. ChatGPT reached only 58\% factual precision on biography generation, and the metric allowed direct comparison of instruction-tuned, chat-tuned, and retrieval-augmented (PerplexityAI) systems. \citet{gao2023} built citation quality into the automatic metrics of ALCE, alongside fluency and correctness, so that citation-grounded systems can be compared reproducibly without depending on commercial search engines.

\subsection{Checking references directly}
Newer tools target bibliographic citations in particular. The RefChecker of \citet{hu2024} breaks model outputs into ``claim-triplets'' and checks them against a reference under zero, noisy, and accurate context. On a curated benchmark of 11,000 triplets, it beat earlier fine-grained checkers by 6.8 to 26.1 points. \citet{aljamaan2024} developed a ``Reference Hallucination Score'' to compare medical chatbots and search tools (ChatGPT, Bing, SciSpace, Elicit, Bard) on several bibliographic items. ChatGPT-3.5 scored worst overall, while Bard gave no references at all for the tested prompts. This shows that ``no citation'' and ``wrong citation'' are separate failures and should be scored separately. For published papers, the separate RefChecker pipeline of \citet{russinovich2026b} checks bibliography entries against arXiv, Semantic Scholar, OpenAlex, CrossRef, DBLP, and the ACL Anthology, and sends unresolved cases to LLM-driven web search. At a few cents per paper, conference-scale auditing is cheap, yet it is still not a standard part of peer review.

\subsection{Checking URLs in agentic systems}
Agentic tools cite live web URLs, so checking them requires an independent historical record. \citet{rao2026} use the Wayback Machine to separate URLs that never existed from URLs that later decayed, since both simply fail to resolve at query time. They also release urlhealth, an open-source tool for checking URL liveness and classifying stale versus hallucinated links. In their self-correction experiments, models given the tool cut non-resolving citation URLs by a factor of 6 to 79, to under 1\%, although the benefit depended on how well a model could use tools. Because this kind of check runs after generation, it can be added to any model or agent.

\subsection{Retrieval as the first line of defense}
Across domains, the most common mitigation is to ground generation in documents retrieved at inference time. This can take the form of classical RAG, commercial ``AI-assisted research'' products \citep{magesh2025}, or multi-step agentic search \citep{du2025,rao2026}. Section~\ref{sec:taxonomy} shows that retrieval lowers fabrication, often by half or more, though it does not remove it. What remains (misattributed support, URL hallucination, and volume effects) still needs the detection methods above, as an addition to retrieval and not a replacement for it.

\section{Research Challenges and Limitations}\label{sec:challenges}
\subsection{Inconsistent definitions and methods}\label{sec:definitions}
Table~\ref{tab:rates} spans about 3\% of URLs to 91\% of references. Some of this range is real, reflecting differences between systems and over time. A good part of it, I would argue, comes from inconsistent definitions. \citet{walters2023} treat fabrication and bibliographic error as separate but related categories, while \citet{russinovich2026b} limit hallucination to identity-level failures and exclude ordinary drift. Verification ranges from manual database lookup \citep{bhattacharyya2023} to automated cross-referencing with LLM-assisted escalation \citep{russinovich2026b} to historical web-archive checks \citep{rao2026}, and each method handles paywalled, recent, or non-English sources differently. Ranking systems across studies is therefore unreliable, and that limitation applies to this synthesis too.

\subsection{Closed commercial systems}
Commercial deep research agents and RAG-based professional tools are closed systems. Their retrieval corpora, ranking logic, and citation-insertion methods are not publicly documented. \citet{magesh2025} note that this makes it hard to say which stage caused a given hallucination: retrieval, synthesis, or citation insertion. Vendors' ``hallucination-free'' claims can only be tested from the outside, through the kind of black-box audit that study carried out. Vendors also change these systems often and without public changelogs, so a benchmark result may be out of date within months.

\subsection{Sensitivity to prompts}
Reported rates shift with prompt wording, requested length, and task framing. \citet{walters2023} note that ChatGPT rarely met the requested word count despite explicit instructions, and \citet{bhattacharyya2023} report rates that varied across prompts for the same system and topic. A benchmark built on a single prompt is therefore weak evidence. Multi-prompt, multi-trial designs are better, but they cost much more to run.

\subsection{A moving target}
LLMs and the products built on them are updated in short cycles, so any benchmark is chasing a moving target. GPT-4's lead over GPT-3.5 \citep{walters2023}, or one agent's lead over another at a single point in time \citep{du2025}, may not hold for later versions. General capability has long-running leaderboards, but no comparable benchmark tracks fabrication rates across model releases.

\subsection{Volume and denominators}\label{sec:volume}
Section~\ref{sec:pattern} showed why percentages alone can mislead for agents. Citation density affects what users actually see. Two systems with the same per-citation rate can expose readers to very different numbers of fabricated citations per report, and \citet{rao2026} show that density and error rate can rise together. Few studies report both the rate and the absolute count. Fewer still report exposure measures such as expected fabricated citations per session, which would say more about real-world risk.

\subsection{Downstream contamination and feedback loops}
Fabricated citations are entering the peer-reviewed literature \citep{russinovich2026b,sakai2026,ansari2026}, and \citet{zhao2026} show that they tend to credit authors who are already prominent. Future LLMs may be trained on this literature, so past hallucinations could reinforce future ones. This is a plausible risk, not a measured effect. Current benchmarks mostly treat the training corpus as fixed and clean, so they cannot detect such a feedback loop.

\section{Research Gaps and Future Directions}\label{sec:gaps}
The challenges above suggest several concrete directions:

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Shared definitions and taxonomies.} The field could adopt the identity-level, drift-excluding definition of \citet{russinovich2026b}, together with the citation-support and citation-precision distinction from \citet{liu2023} and \citet{gao2023}. Studies could then report existence, attribute accuracy, and support accuracy separately, in place of one blended ``hallucination rate.''
\item \textbf{Continuously updated, cross-vendor benchmarks for agentic search.} DeepResearch Bench \citep{du2025} and the URL verification method of \citet{rao2026} are good starting points. A versioned leaderboard built on them would show whether vendors' claims of improvement hold up over time, and it would help separate real gains from benchmark overfitting.
\item \textbf{Independent ground truth for web-citing systems.} The Wayback Machine approach of \citet{rao2026} should be extended and standardized. Of the methods reviewed here, it is the one that cleanly separates fabrication from link rot, and most audits still lack that distinction.
\item \textbf{Volume-aware and exposure-based metrics.} Benchmarks should report absolute fabricated-citation counts and user-facing exposure estimates next to percentages, especially for agents, whose citation density varies widely (Section~\ref{sec:volume}).
\item \textbf{Cross-lingual and cross-domain coverage.} Most of the studies reviewed here focus on English-language, Western academic or legal sources. \citet{mcgowan2023} is a partial exception, with Spanish and Italian queries. Given the domain effects in Section~\ref{sec:domain}, and the global use of agentic tools, multilingual and multi-jurisdictional benchmarks are an obvious gap.
\item \textbf{Feedback-loop and corpus-contamination studies.} Longitudinal work should test whether training corpora containing LLM-fabricated citations \citep{zhao2026} make later models less reliable. Benchmarks that assume a clean ground truth cannot answer this.
\item \textbf{Automated verification in peer review and publishing.} Conference-scale auditing costs only cents per paper \citep{russinovich2026b}, so tools like this could be built into submission and review systems. Research should also measure their false-positive and false-negative rates at scale, and their effect on reviewers and authors.
\item \textbf{Transparency or standard audit protocols for commercial agentic tools.} Given the opacity described in Section~\ref{sec:challenges}, the field needs vendor-supported evaluation APIs or agreed third-party audit protocols. Protocols modeled on \citet{magesh2025} and \citet{rao2026} could allow independent, reproducible benchmarking even without vendor cooperation.
\end{itemize}

\section{Synthesis of Evidence}\label{sec:synthesis}
No single ``hallucination rate'' describes this literature. Unaugmented models can produce many fabricated or inaccurate references in some domains. Retrieval and agentic search reduce some of these failures, but they raise new questions about citation support, URL validity, and citation volume. The strongest studies measure these properties separately. Figure~\ref{fig:taxonomy} shows the distinction, and Table~\ref{tab:mitigation} shows why no single mitigation covers every layer.

I draw three conclusions. First, a reported percentage means little without its system, domain, prompt, denominator, and verification protocol. Second, agent studies should report citation accuracy and citation quantity together, because Equation~\eqref{eq:volume-effect} ties them to the number of bad citations a reader actually receives. Third, external verification is now practical. Recent audits of both scholarly references and web URLs show that it can be done at scale, so it can sit alongside model-side safeguards.

\section{Conclusion}\label{sec:conclusion}
Citation fabrication is well documented, measurable, and largely unsolved. The evidence shows a real but uneven improvement. Early chat models often fabricated more than half of their references. Later models and retrieval-augmented professional tools did noticeably better, in several studies cutting the rate by half or more. The best deep research agents can pass 90\% citation accuracy on curated benchmarks, yet independent audits still find fabricated URLs, and their high citation volume means that those errors add up within a single report. The damage is no longer limited to individual users. Fabricated citations are in the proceedings of leading AI conferences and across the wider scholarly corpus, and future models may be trained on that record. Better models and better retrieval will help, but they are not enough on their own. The field also needs shared definitions, external ground truth, volume-aware metrics, and reproducible cross-vendor audits. Until then, it will keep producing useful studies that are hard to compare, and it will struggle to say with confidence whether citations are getting more reliable.
